How to Learn While You Scroll: Turn Short Videos Into Things You Remember

You watch dozens of useful TikToks, Reels and Shorts a day and forget almost all of them. A simple, research-backed method for turning scrolling into real learning.

By the Sparkstr teamUpdated 22 November 20268 min read

The short version

Watching a video feels like learning, but most of it is gone within days. To keep it:

  1. Save on purpose, with one line on why.
  2. Write the key point in your own words.
  3. Test yourself instead of rewatching.
  4. Come back to it a day later, then a week later.
  5. Use it within 48 hours.

Why you forget what you watch

Short videos are brilliant at making things feel clear. A chef flips an omelette, a coach shows a stretch, an accountant explains a tax rule in 40 seconds, and you think "got it." Then the next video starts, and the one after that.

Three things work against you:

  • Feeling it isn't learning it. Psychologists call it the fluency illusion. When information is easy to take in, we overestimate how well we'll remember it. Smooth, well-edited videos are the most fluent content there is.
  • Interference. Each new video pushes out the last one before your brain has had a chance to store it. Fifty videos in a row means very little of any single one survives.
  • No second contact. Memory fades fast after one exposure. Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped this "forgetting curve" in the 1880s, and it still holds. Things you see once and never come back to fade the fastest.

The good news is that the fixes are small, and the research behind them is some of the most solid in learning science.

The 5-step method

1. Save on purpose, and say why

Don't save everything that's interesting. Save what you'd actually do, use or teach. A good filter: "Will I want this again in a month?"

When you save, add one short line on why. "Try this for Sunday dinner." "Use this hook in my next post." "Ask the GP about this." That one line does two jobs. It forces a moment of thought, and it gives you the words you'll search for later.

2. Write the key point in your own words

Before you scroll on, write the main idea in a sentence or two, without copying the caption. This is the generation effect: information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you only read. Researchers Slamecka and Graf showed this back in 1978, and it has been replicated many times since.

A simple template works well:

The idea: (one sentence)
The steps or numbers: (only what you'd need to do it)
When I'll use it: (a real moment)

3. Test yourself instead of rewatching

This is the single most powerful step, and the one people skip. When you come back to a saved video, don't press play first. Look at the title or your note and try to recall the steps from memory. Then check.

It's called retrieval practice. In a well-known 2006 study, Roediger and Karpicke found that students who tested themselves on a passage remembered much more a week later than students who simply re-read it, even though re-reading felt more effective at the time. Struggling to remember is the part that does the work.

4. Come back to it later, more than once

Spreading reviews out beats cramming them together. This is the spacing effect, one of the most replicated findings in psychology. A large review by Cepeda and colleagues in 2006 found spaced practice reliably beat massed practice across hundreds of studies.

You don't need flashcard software to use it. A simple rhythm is enough:

  • The next day: a 10-second recall of anything you saved yesterday.
  • A few days later: recall it again.
  • A week later: in your weekly review (below).

5. Use it within 48 hours

The fastest way to remember a recipe is to cook it. The same goes for a workout, a spreadsheet shortcut or a phrase in another language. Doing something once turns a fragile memory into an experience, which is far harder to forget. If you can't use it soon, ask whether it really needed saving.

Train your feed to teach you

Your feed shows you more of whatever you engage with. You can use that on purpose:

  • Search, don't just scroll. Search for the topic you want to learn ("beginner sourdough", "Excel tips for accountants") and watch a few results all the way through. The algorithm takes the hint quickly.
  • Follow a few great teachers. Creators who explain clearly and show their working are worth ten who just entertain.
  • Use "Not interested." It's the fastest way to clear out noise.
  • Watch with a question. Going in with "how do I fix my squat depth?" makes you notice the useful parts instead of drifting.
  • Check claims that matter. For health, money or legal tips, treat a video as a starting point and check a trusted source before acting.

The 10-minute weekly review

Once a week, with a coffee, go through what you saved:

  1. Recall first. For each item, try to remember the key point before opening it.
  2. Keep, use or delete. If it's still useful, leave it. If you've used it, great. If you've lost interest, delete it. A smaller library is a more useful one.
  3. Sort into folders like "Recipes", "Fitness", "Work", "Gifts". Folders turn a pile of videos into a reference library.
  4. Pick one to act on this week. Just one.

What it looks like in practice

A recipe. You see a 30-second one-pan dinner. You save it with "Tuesday dinner, have all ingredients except feta." Your note says "Orzo + stock + spinach, 15 min, feta at the end." On Tuesday you cook it from your note and only check the video once. Now you know it.

A work tip. A video shows a keyboard shortcut that saves you five minutes a day. You write it down, try it immediately, and recall it the next morning before opening the video. By Friday you don't need the video.

A language phrase. You save a Reel explaining a Spanish expression. Your note is the phrase and when to use it. The next day you try to say it out loud before checking. A week later it comes back on its own.

Frequently asked questions

Can you actually learn from TikTok and Reels?

Yes, if you treat them as a starting point. Short videos are great for discovering ideas and quick techniques. The learning happens when you save the good ones, put them in your own words, recall them later and use them.

Why do I forget videos so quickly?

Because one fast exposure, followed by dozens more, gives your memory nothing to hold on to. Smooth videos also create a feeling of understanding that's stronger than the actual memory. Recalling and revisiting fixes both.

How often should I review saved videos?

A quick recall the next day, again a few days later, and once more in a weekly review is plenty for most things. Spaced reviews like this beat watching the same video five times in a row.

Is rewatching a video a good way to remember it?

It's better than nothing, but trying to recall the content before rewatching works much better. The effort of remembering is what strengthens the memory.

What's the best way to organise videos I want to learn from?
